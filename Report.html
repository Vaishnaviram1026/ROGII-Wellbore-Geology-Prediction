1. Executive Summary

During horizontal drilling, geologists and geosteering specialists use well trajectory and gamma-ray measurements to understand the wellbore's position relative to geological layers. Accurate estimates of True Vertical Thickness (TVT) can help inform geosteering decisions and potentially reduce unnecessary drilling corrections.

This project investigates whether a supervised machine learning approach can predict TVT beyond the Prediction Start (PS) point more accurately than simple geological baseline methods. A linear regression model was developed using engineered features from well trajectory, gamma-ray measurements, and historical TVT information. Its performance was compared with two baseline approaches: holding the last known TVT constant and extrapolating the recent TVT trend.

The model was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation grouped by well. It achieved an RMSE of 14.84 feet, compared with 15.61 feet for the hold-last-TVT baseline, representing a 4.90% reduction in prediction error. The model performed better than the hold baseline for approximately 60% of evaluated wells. However, its advantage was modest, and predictions became less reliable at greater distances beyond Prediction Start.

The findings suggest that linear regression offers a measurable but limited improvement over a simple baseline. The recommended next step is to use the model as a supplementary decision-support tool, with geologists reviewing predictions alongside geological measurements rather than relying on the model alone.

Although improved predictions could potentially help reduce drilling time and associated rig costs, this project does not measure actual operational or financial savings. Further validation using real drilling outcomes would be required before recommending operational deployment.

2. Business Context and Objective

2.1 Business Context

Horizontal drilling requires geologists and geosteering specialists to continuously interpret geological measurements and wellbore trajectories. Their goal is to understand the wellbore's position relative to geological layers and support decisions that keep drilling aligned with the intended target interval.

Gamma-ray (GR) measurements and well trajectory information provide useful geological signals, but estimating True Vertical Thickness (TVT) along an extended horizontal section remains challenging. Inaccurate estimates can increase uncertainty when interpreting the wellbore's position and deciding whether drilling adjustments may be necessary.

This project examines how machine learning can support this interpretation process by predicting TVT beyond the Prediction Start (PS) point, where historical TVT information is available but subsequent TVT values must be estimated.

2.2 Business Objective

The primary business objective is to support better-informed geosteering decisions that could reduce avoidable drilling time and associated rig costs.

The project aims to improve TVT prediction accuracy compared with simple baseline methods. More accurate predictions could provide geologists with additional information when assessing geological changes and potential drilling adjustments.

The project does not directly measure drilling efficiency, rig time, or financial savings. Therefore, any potential operational benefit is considered a future opportunity rather than a demonstrated outcome.

2.3 Stakeholders

The primary stakeholders are geologists and geosteering specialists, who interpret geological measurements and support drilling-direction decisions.

Drilling engineers are secondary stakeholders because geological interpretations can inform operational planning and drilling adjustments.

The proposed model is intended as a decision-support tool. Its predictions would supplement professional geological interpretation rather than automatically control drilling operations.

2.4 Success Criteria

Technical success is evaluated using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), measured in feet, for TVT predictions on held-out wells.

The linear regression model is compared with two baseline approaches: holding the last known TVT constant and extrapolating the recent TVT trend. Additional evaluation examines performance across individual wells and at increasing distances beyond Prediction Start.

From a business perspective, meaningful success would require demonstrating that improved TVT estimates help geosteering specialists make better decisions and reduce avoidable drilling time or costs. Establishing that impact would require operational validation beyond the available dataset.

4. Topic and Industry Context

4.1 Geosteering in Horizontal Drilling

In the oil and gas industry, horizontal drilling allows operators to extend a wellbore through geological formations to access a larger portion of a target reservoir. However, underground rock layers are not always uniform, and their exact positions cannot be known with complete certainty before drilling.

Geosteering is the process of interpreting geological and drilling measurements to help guide the wellbore through the intended formation. Geologists and geosteering specialists use information such as well trajectory, gamma-ray (GR) measurements, and geological interpretations to assess the wellbore's position and recommend adjustments when necessary.

4.2 Importance of TVT Prediction

True Vertical Thickness (TVT) is a geological measurement associated with the thickness of a formation in the vertical direction. In the ROGII prediction task, the goal is to estimate TVT values along a horizontal wellbore beyond a designated Prediction Start (PS) point.

Reliable TVT predictions can provide additional information about geological changes ahead of the currently interpreted section. This information may help specialists evaluate wellbore placement and make more informed geosteering decisions.

However, prediction is challenging because geological formations vary between wells, and relationships between trajectory measurements, gamma-ray signals, and TVT may not remain consistent over long distances.

4.3 Machine Learning Approach

Machine learning offers a way to identify relationships between available wellbore measurements and historical geological interpretations.

For this project, linear regression was selected as the predictive modeling method. It provides a relatively simple and interpretable approach for estimating TVT changes using engineered numerical features.

The model uses information derived from well trajectory, gamma-ray measurements, and the last known TVT value at Prediction Start. Its performance is compared against two simple prediction strategies: maintaining the last known TVT and extending the recent TVT trend.

Linear regression is suitable for this project because it establishes whether a straightforward statistical model can improve prediction accuracy before considering more complex approaches.

4.4 Industry Relevance

More accurate geological predictions could support drilling teams by providing an additional source of information when evaluating wellbore placement. If future operational studies demonstrate that better predictions reduce unnecessary corrections or drilling delays, the approach could contribute to improved drilling efficiency.

Nevertheless, prediction accuracy alone does not establish operational value. Any practical application would require geological review, validation on additional wells, and evidence that the predictions improve actual drilling decisions.

5. Data and Preparation

5.1 Data Source

This project uses the ROGII Wellbore Geology Prediction dataset, accessed through Kaggle. The dataset contains horizontal wellbore information, including trajectory measurements, gamma-ray (GR) readings, and geological interpretations represented by True Vertical Thickness (TVT).

The training data provides historical TVT information and identifies a Prediction Start (PS) point for each well. The objective is to use the available information to predict TVT values beyond that point.

The original dataset is stored locally under data/raw/. Instructions for obtaining the data are provided in the project's GitHub README.

5.2 Data Exploration

Exploratory data analysis was performed to understand the structure of the wellbore data and identify factors that could affect prediction accuracy.

The analysis examined well profiles, TVT patterns, trajectory measurements, and gamma-ray information. Visualizations were generated to inspect differences between wells and assess the performance of simple baseline predictions.

These observations informed the selection of features and the decision to evaluate the model on wells that were not included in its training folds.

5.3 Data Cleaning, Preparation, and Feature Engineering

Data cleaning and preparation were performed using Python and pandas. The workflow checked each horizontal well for required columns, converted measurements to numeric values, and validated the trajectory coordinates and Prediction Start structure. Wells were required to contain at least 10 known TVT observations before Prediction Start.

Missing gamma-ray readings were imputed using the median GR value from a historical window of up to 500 feet before Prediction Start. A separate indicator identified missing GR measurements. When insufficient historical information was available to estimate the recent TVT trend, the slope was set to zero.

Rows without valid TVT target values were excluded from model training and validation. The workflow also checked that the engineered features and training targets contained valid numerical values. Wells that failed the preparation checks were reported, and training was stopped rather than silently excluding them.

Seven numerical features were created from trajectory changes, gamma-ray measurements, and historical TVT trends. These features were standardized using StandardScaler within the linear regression pipeline.

For model development, a maximum of 2,000 prediction observations per well was used to manage computational requirements. The final prepared dataset contained 773 wells and 1,542,405 observations.

The preprocessing focused on data validation, targeted missing-value handling, and feature engineering. Duplicate removal and extensive outlier filtering were not performed.

5.4 Training and Validation Design

The model was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation grouped by well, implemented with GroupKFold.

Grouping by well ensures that observations from the same well do not appear in both the training and validation portions of a fold. This provides a more realistic evaluation of how the model performs on unseen wells.

To manage the size of the development dataset, the workflow applied a cap of 2,000 validation observations per well.

The evaluation covered 773 wells and 1,542,405 validation observations.

5.5 Data Limitations

The available measurements do not provide a complete description of underground geological conditions. Geological patterns can also differ substantially between wells, making predictions more difficult when the model encounters unfamiliar formations.

Additionally, prediction accuracy may decrease farther beyond Prediction Start, where the available historical TVT information becomes less representative of subsequent geological changes.

These limitations are considered when interpreting model performance and assessing potential operational use.

6. Method and Implementation

6.1 Modeling Approach

The project uses supervised linear regression to predict True Vertical Thickness (TVT) beyond the Prediction Start (PS) point of each horizontal well.

The model predicts the change in TVT relative to the last known TVT value rather than predicting absolute TVT directly:

Predicted TVT = Last known TVT + Predicted change in TVT

This approach uses the known geological interpretation at Prediction Start as a reference for estimating subsequent TVT values.

6.2 Data Preparation and Feature Engineering

The ROGII wellbore dataset was obtained through Kaggle and processed using Python and pandas. The preparation workflow organizes well measurements, identifies the Prediction Start point, and handles missing gamma-ray information.

Seven engineered features were created from trajectory measurements, gamma-ray readings, and historical TVT information:

dMD, dZ, dX, dY, GR_dev, GR_missing, and slope_x_dMD.

These features describe changes in wellbore position, gamma-ray behavior, and the recent TVT trend.

6.3 Model Training and Evaluation

The prediction model was implemented using a scikit-learn pipeline consisting of StandardScaler and LinearRegression.

Five-fold cross-validation grouped by well was used to evaluate performance on unseen wells. The model was compared with two baseline approaches: holding the last known TVT constant and extending the recent TVT trend.

The linear regression model achieved an RMSE of 14.84 feet, compared with 15.61 feet for the hold-last-TVT baseline, representing a 4.90% improvement. However, the model performed less reliably at greater distances beyond Prediction Start.

Detailed evaluation results are presented in Section 7.

7. Evaluation and Findings

7.1 Evaluation Strategy

The linear regression model was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation grouped by well (GroupKFold). This approach ensured that each validation fold contained wells that were not included in the corresponding training fold.

The evaluation covered 773 wells and 1,542,405 validation observations.

Model performance was assessed using three metrics:

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): Measures prediction error in feet, with greater emphasis on large errors.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Measures the average absolute prediction error in feet.

R-squared (R²): Measures how much variation in the observed TVT values is explained by the model.

RMSE was used as the primary comparison metric because large geological prediction errors can be particularly important when interpreting wellbore placement.

7.2 Model Performance Compared with Baselines

The linear regression model was compared with two baseline approaches: holding the last known TVT constant and extrapolating the recent TVT trend.

Table 7.1. Cross-Validation Performance Comparison

Prediction MethodRMSE (ft)MAE (ft)
Hold-last-TVT baseline15.6110.95
Linear extrapolation baseline149.3157.18
Linear regression model14.8410.47

Table 7.1 interpretation: Linear regression achieved the lowest overall RMSE and MAE among the three methods. Its RMSE was 4.90% lower than the hold-last-TVT baseline, indicating a modest improvement in prediction accuracy. Linear extrapolation performed substantially worse, suggesting that extending recent TVT trends without adjustment can produce large errors.

The linear regression model achieved an R² of 0.088, indicating that it explained only a small proportion of the variation in observed TVT values. This highlights the difficulty of predicting geological changes from the available features.

7.3 Performance Across Wells

The linear regression model outperformed the hold-last-TVT baseline on 464 of 773 wells (60.03%).

This indicates that the model provided better predictions for a majority of evaluated wells. However, approximately 40% of wells did not benefit from the model relative to the hold baseline.

The results demonstrate that the improvement was not consistent across all geological conditions.

7.4 Performance by Distance Beyond Prediction Start

Figure 7.1. Out-of-Fold RMSE by Distance from Prediction Start. Comparison of prediction errors for the hold-last-TVT baseline, linear extrapolation baseline, and linear regression model across five distance bands.

Interpretation: Linear regression generally achieves lower RMSE than the hold baseline at shorter prediction distances. However, beyond 5,000 feet, its RMSE increases to 21.06 feet, compared with 19.86 feet for the hold baseline. Linear extrapolation produces substantially larger errors, especially at longer distances. These results indicate that prediction accuracy decreases with distance and that the regression model does not consistently outperform the simpler baseline.

7.5 Actual Versus Predicted TVT

A comparison of observed and predicted TVT values was generated to examine how closely model predictions followed the actual geological interpretations.

RMSE and MAE provide a more informative assessment of model performance.

7.6 Key Findings

The evaluation produced three main findings:

Linear regression improves overall accuracy: The model reduced RMSE by 4.90% compared with holding the last known TVT constant.

Performance varies across wells: The model outperformed the hold baseline on approximately 60% of evaluated wells, but not all wells.

Long-distance predictions remain challenging: Beyond 5,000 feet from Prediction Start, the hold baseline performed better than the regression model.

Overall, the results demonstrate that linear regression offers a measurable but limited improvement over a simple geological baseline. The model is better suited for supplementary geological interpretation than independent operational decision-making.

8. Advanced Component and Reproducibility

8.1 Advanced Component: Well-Grouped Cross-Validation

The project uses five-fold cross-validation with GroupKFold to evaluate the linear regression model on unseen wells. The labeled training dataset contains 773 wells. In each fold, approximately 80% of the wells are used for training and 20% for validation, with no well appearing in both groups.

This approach evaluates the model across all 773 wells rather than relying on a single train-validation split. The resulting out-of-fold predictions were used to calculate performance metrics and generate evaluation figures.

8.2 Saving and Reloading the Model

After training, the linear regression pipeline was saved using joblib as:

artifacts/rogii_tvt_core_model.joblib

The saved pipeline contains both StandardScaler and LinearRegression, allowing the same preprocessing and prediction steps to be reused without retraining.

To demonstrate that the saved model works independently of the training notebook, a separate script was developed:

src/load_and_test.py

The script loads the saved model, processes the Kaggle test wells, generates TVT predictions, and saves the results as CSV files.

The following outputs were generated:

8.3 Reproducing the Project

The complete source code, saved model, dependencies, and supporting results are available in the GitHub repository.

Detailed instructions for downloading the Kaggle dataset, setting up the Python environment, reproducing model training and evaluation, and generating predictions using the saved model are provided in the repository's README.md.

Users can also apply the saved model to their own horizontal-well datasets, provided the data follows the expected CSV format and includes the required fields: MD, X, Y, Z, GR, and TVT_input. The TVT_input column must contain at least 10 known historical TVT observations followed by missing values for the prediction interval. Users may need to adapt the data-loading script to match their own file names and folder structure.

The saved model can generate TVT predictions for compatible datasets without retraining. However, prediction accuracy may vary depending on the geological characteristics and quality of the new data.

9. Recommendations, Limitations, and Next Steps

9.1 Recommendations

The linear regression model achieved an RMSE of 14.84 feet, compared with 15.61 feet for the hold-last-TVT baseline, representing a 4.90% improvement. It also outperformed the hold baseline on 464 of 773 wells (60.03%).

Based on these findings, the model is recommended as a supplementary decision-support tool for geologists and geosteering specialists, rather than a replacement for geological expertise.

Predicted TVT values could be reviewed alongside gamma-ray measurements, well trajectory information, and existing geological interpretations. The model should not be used to make automatic drilling adjustments.

9.2 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered before applying the model operationally:

Modest improvement: Although linear regression outperformed the hold baseline overall, the improvement was relatively small.

Variation across wells: The model did not outperform the hold baseline on every well.

Long-distance prediction errors: Beyond 5,000 feet from Prediction Start, linear regression had a higher RMSE (21.06 feet) than the hold baseline (19.86 feet).

Limited geological information: The model relies on trajectory measurements, gamma-ray data, and historical TVT rather than a complete description of underground geological conditions.

No operational validation: The model was evaluated using historical data, not through live drilling decisions.

These limitations indicate that the model's predictions should be interpreted cautiously, particularly at longer distances beyond Prediction Start.

9.3 Potential Business Value

More accurate TVT predictions could help geosteering specialists identify geological changes and make better-informed drilling decisions. If future testing demonstrates that the predictions reduce unnecessary drilling corrections or delays, the approach could potentially save drilling time and associated rig costs.

For illustration, assuming a rig operating cost of $20,000 per day, avoiding two hours of drilling time would represent approximately:

Potential savings = ($20,000 ÷ 24 hours) × 2 hours = $1,667 per well

This calculation is hypothetical and is not an observed project result. The analysis did not measure drilling time saved, operational decisions changed, or actual financial benefits.

9.4 Next Steps

The recommended next step is to conduct a supervised pilot evaluation in which geologists compare model predictions with their existing geological interpretations.

The pilot should measure whether predictions improve decision quality, particularly at shorter distances beyond Prediction Start. It should also record cases where the model performs worse than the hold baseline.

If the pilot demonstrates consistent value, future work could explore additional geological information, improved model features, and integration into an operational review workflow.

Any operational deployment should retain human oversight and establish clear procedures for handling uncertain or inaccurate predictions.

9.5 Conclusion

This project demonstrates that a relatively simple linear regression model can improve TVT prediction accuracy compared with a hold-last-TVT baseline, although the improvement is modest and varies across wells and prediction distances.

The results support further investigation of the model as a geological decision-support resource, but they do not justify autonomous geosteering or claims of measured drilling cost savings.
